We introduce a benchmark that measures formalization
faithfulness deterministically, with neither a human grader
nor a language-model as a judge. We start from a formal statement
and \textit{informalize} it, with a deterministic template
renderer, into a natural-language story. A model is shown
the story and asked to recover the statement. We use the
benchmark to study how models should be asked to formalize.
With reasoning disabled, splitting the task into an
abstraction step followed by formalization closes most
of the gap between one-shot formalization of the story
and formalization of a literal description written by
the renderer itself.

Our main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce a benchmark using the Equational
    Theories Project (ETP)~\citep{bolan2025etp}, which maps
    implications of magma operation equations. A model's
    answer is parsed and compared with the source modulo
    a small, enumerated group of symmetries. The known
    truth values of the implications are deliberately
    unused, because the benchmark scores the translation
    of a claim, not reasoning about whether the claim holds.

    \item We study a two-stage route to formalization.
    In the first stage, the model performs an abstraction
    step from natural language to natural language:
    it rewrites the story to discard the narrative and
    keep only the logical core. In the second stage,
    it formalizes that abstraction into our target
    language. We compare this route with one-shot
    formalization of the story and of the renderer's
    literal description.
\end{itemize}

The two-stage results suggest that separating abstraction
from formalization can help models recover the logical
content of a story. By starting from formal statements
and manufacturing their informal descriptions, the
benchmark makes it possible to study this process with
deterministic grading.

\paragraph{Informalization for data generation}
The strongest current autoformalizers are language models fine-tuned on formal-informal pairs.~\cite{gao2025herald,kimina2025,lin2025goedel}. Oftentimes, their training data is manufactured by having an LLM informalize an existing formal library.~\cite{jiang2023mma,gao2025herald,ying2024leanworkbook,xin2024deepseekprover}. We use informalization for the opposite purpose: performed by a deterministic renderer rather than a model, it yields an evaluation oracle instead of training data. This enables an injective mapping from  informal stories to  formal sentences.  
 
\paragraph{Measuring faithfulness.}
Type-checking, BLEU, and exact match are blind to meaning and correlate poorly with human judgment~\cite{wu2022autoformalization,azerbayev2023proofnet,poiroux2025reliable}. The alternatives can be sorted by what they trust. Human-labelled sets such as ProofNetVerif~\cite{poiroux2025reliable}, ConsistencyCheck~\cite{chen2025reform}, CriticLeanBench~\cite{criticlean2025}, and MA-Align~\cite{mathatlas2026} allow metrics to be meta-evaluated, but they are expensive to build, and the references they rest on are themselves unreliable: annotation found 16.4\% of the human-written formal statements in miniF2F and 38.5\% of those in ProofNet to be semantically wrong~\cite{chen2025reform}. Model-based methods trust a language model as a judge~\cite{ying2024leanworkbook,lin2025goedel,kimina2025}, a trained critic~\cite{lu2025formalalign,criticlean2025,mathesis2025}, or a back-translator~\cite{patel2024newsymbol,li2024symbolic}; the best judges agree with experts about 86\% of the time~\cite{chen2025reform}, and in specification autoformalization a judge missed a quarter of the failures that an executable check caught~\cite{verusspecgym2026}. Prover-based methods trust an automated prover to establish equivalence with a reference~\cite{liu2025rethinking,poiroux2025reliable,murphy2024leaneuclid,liu2025gted}, and so need both a reference and a proof search that may time out. Every method above needs a human, a model, or an incomplete prover somewhere in the loop. We reverse the direction of translation so that it is available by construction. The price is a narrower criterion, feasible only because the target language is small.
 
\paragraph{Synthetic language with exact ground truth.}
Synthetic and semi-synthetic reasoning benchmarks construct
natural-language problems from controlled underlying
structures~\citep{weston2015babi,sinha2019clutrr,clark2020ruletaker,tafjord2021proofwriter}.
ZebraLogic uses clue templates~\citep{zebralogic2025},
whereas SATBench uses LLMs to render SAT formulas as
narratives~\citep{satbench2025}. FOLIO combines expert-written
and template-assisted stories and also evaluates
natural-language-to-first-order-logic translation~\citep{han2022folio}.
Newer formal projects have been used to reduce contamination
risk in autoformalization evaluation~\citep{poiroux2025reliable}.
We use deterministic renderers to produce paired story and
literal descriptions, then score how accurately models recover
the source statements under the accepted symmetries. Models
are not asked to determine whether the implications hold.
 
\paragraph{Staged formalization.}
Passing through an intermediate representation on the way to a formal target is an old idea in semantic parsing, whether a canonical utterance~\cite{shin2021constrained} or an intermediate query language~\cite{guo2019irnet,gan2021natsql}. Prompting methods that first abstract a problem~\cite{zheng2023stepback} or draft an informal sketch~\cite{jiang2023dsp} follow the same pattern, and formalize-then-solve pipelines identify translation as a major source of error~\cite{olausson2023linc,pan2023logiclm,ye2023satlm,lyu2023faithfulcot}, motivating decomposition of the translation step~\cite{ryu2024divide}. The remedies proposed for autoformalization add a verifier or a feedback loop: reflective refinement~\cite{chen2025reform}, faithfulness-guided decoding~\cite{mohammad2026faithfulness}, or reranking candidates by symbolic equivalence~\cite{li2024symbolic}. Our two-stage route uses neither. It changes only how the model is asked, and it composes with any of them.

We should just start with the main point, and into describing the dataset creation} Faithfulness is hard to grade because grading requires already knowing what
the informal text meant. The benchmark obtains that knowledge by
construction: instead of collecting informal mathematics and debating what
its formalization should have been, it starts from the formal statement and
\textit{manufactures} the informal text, with a renderer whose output
provably determines the statement it came from. Grading a model's answer
then reduces to comparing two formal objects, which is mechanical. Every
benchmark item is produced and scored in four steps.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sample a statement.} An item is an ordered pair $(E, F)$
    of equational laws, read as the claim ``every structure satisfying $E$
    also satisfies $F$.''
    \item \textbf{Informalize.} A deterministic renderer turns the pair into
    a short themed story: a workplace whose custom always upholds $E$,
    ending with the open question of whether a second regularity, $F$, must
    also hold. The story contains no notation, no variable letters, and no
    mathematical vocabulary.
    \item \textbf{Formalize.} The model is shown only the story, together
    with a self-contained prompt that defines a minimal formal notation, and
    is asked to state the assumed law and the questioned law in that
    notation.
    \item \textbf{Grade.} A parser reads the model's answer, canonicalizes
    it, and compares it with the source pair modulo an enumerated group of
    symmetries. The verdict is \textit{correct}, \textit{wrong} (well-formed
    but denoting a different statement), or \textit{unparseable}.
\end{enumerate}
No human and no language model appears anywhere on the reference side.
Rendering and grading are pure functions: the same pair always produces the
byte-identical story, the same response always receives the same verdict,
and every score is exactly re-derivable from the model's raw output.

\subsection{Substrate: implications between equational laws}
A \textit{magma} is a set equipped with a single binary operation
$\diamond$ and no axioms at all. A \textit{law} is a universally quantified
identity between terms of that operation, such as
$x \diamond y = (y \diamond y) \diamond x$; since nothing (not even
associativity) is assumed, terms are fully parenthesized and the shape of
the nesting is part of the meaning. The Equational Theories Project
catalogued the 4{,}694 laws with at most four applications of the operation
and resolved all 22{,}028{,}942 ordered implications between them, with the
complete graph verified in Lean [cite]. However, our benchmark is not concerned with the truth value of the implications. 
Three properties make this an unusually clean substrate for measuring
translation. First, equality of meaning is decidable: whether two answers
in this fragment state the same law is a small computation
(Section~\ref{sec:grading}), whereas for mathematics at large that question
can be as hard as the mathematics itself. This is what lets the benchmark
dispense with judges. Second, difficulty is controllable, with two
structural dials: the number of operation applications and the maximum
nesting depth. Third, the laws are uniform and obscure: beyond a handful
with established names (commutativity, idempotence), they are nameless and
essentially absent from natural-language text, so a model cannot retrieve a
memorized formalization, it must read the story in front of it.
The two zero-operation laws ($x = x$ and
$x = y$) are excluded everywhere.\footnote{A law with no operation offers
nothing to translate, and models misread exactly these: they embellish the
trivial law by inventing an operation the text never states. Pairs
containing one also distort difficulty stratification, since all of the
pair's operations then sit in the partner law.}
\subsection{Informalization: rendering a statement as a story}
Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-item} shows a complete item. The renderer maps
each law to a narrated procedure. Variables become ingredients from the
theme's fixed palette (crimson, ochre, \dots), assigned by order of first
appearance. Each application of the operation becomes one step of a
concrete, order-sensitive action, and every intermediate result is given a
name (``Batch 1''), so nesting is expressed by reference to earlier steps
rather than by nested prose. Universal quantification is narrated
explicitly (``take any two pigments at all,'' ``however she chooses'').
The assumed law $E$ is presented as an exceptionless custom of the
workplace; the questioned law $F$ is then posed as an open question,
quantified afresh, in one direction only, and left unanswered.
Four themes supply the vocabulary --- pouring pigments (\textit{paint}),
brewing over leaves (\textit{tea}), grafting cuttings (\textit{graft}),
relaying signals (\textit{signal}) --- while the sentence skeleton is
identical across them. Every
action phrasing is order-sensitive by design (``pours crimson \textit{into}
ochre''); symmetric wordings such as ``mix'' are banned, because they would
silently assert a commutativity the law may not have.
 \textit{Injectivity}: a back-parser recovers
both term trees, the ingredient-to-variable correspondence, and every
argument order from the story text alone. Injectivity is what makes the task well-posed:
rendering loses no information needed to recover the statement, so a wrong
answer is attributable to the model, not to ambiguity in the text.
We use a template renderer rather than a language model deliberately.
Informalizing with a model would inject, at generation time, exactly the
infidelity the benchmark is meant to measure.
\subsection{The formalization task}
The prompt shown with each story is self-contained and defines the entire
target language on the page: an expression is an ingredient name or
$\mathtt{op}(\textit{first}, \textit{second})$, where \texttt{op} stands
for one performance of the story's combining action on its two inputs in a
fixed order; an equation asserts that two expressions always come out the
same; and the answer is exactly two lines,
\begin{quote}
\texttt{ASSUME: <equation for the custom that always holds>}\\
\texttt{ASK: <equation for the regularity the question asks about>}
\end{quote}
Its worked example uses a fifth setting (a bookbinder stacking volumes) that
never occurs in the corpus. Nothing about Lean, magmas, or the source
project is assumed or mentioned.
\subsection{Grading}
\label{sec:grading}
The grader extracts the last \texttt{ASSUME:} and \texttt{ASK:} lines from
the raw response and parses each in the tiny prefix grammar. If extraction
or parsing fails --- a missing line, malformed syntax, runaway nesting ---
the verdict is \textit{unparseable}. Otherwise both equations are
canonicalized by renaming variables in order of first appearance, and the
answer is compared with the source pair under all eight combinations of
three commuting involutions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item swapping the two sides of the assumed equation;
    \item swapping the two sides of the asked equation;
    \item dualizing both equations, i.e.\ mirroring the argument order of
    every \texttt{op} application.
\end{itemize}
The answer is \textit{correct} if some combination maps it onto the source
pair, and the minimal matching transform is recorded with the verdict, so
exact-convention answers remain distinguishable from swapped or dualized
ones.
Each accepted symmetry is a faithful reading of the story.
Ingredient names are arbitrary labels. ``Batch 1 and Batch 3 always come
out the same color'' is symmetric, so the order of an equation's sides is
not recoverable intent. And the story cannot say which participant of
``pours crimson into ochre'' is the operation's \textit{first} argument; an
answer that adopts the opposite convention uniformly states the dual law
--- the same claim with every application mirrored. However, any
structural difference --- one transposed pair of arguments, one invented or
omitted application --- makes the answer \textit{wrong}.
\textit{Wrong} is the verdict of interest: a well-formed answer denoting a
different statement is semantic drift, observed directly rather than
estimated by a judge. Keeping \textit{unparseable} separate matters because
the failures differ in kind --- a model that cannot hold the output format
is not the same as a model that writes fluent formal syntax meaning the
wrong thing.

\subsection{A second rendering: the literal arm}
Every pair can also be rendered by a second deterministic renderer that
skips the disguise. The \textit{literal} arm produces a direct description
that openly speaks of an operation with a first and a second input and uses
letters for the objects, with the same one-step-per-application discipline:
``apply the operation to $x$ as its first input and $y$ as its second
input, and call the result Value 1; then \dots''. The description is
question-framed like the story, injective like the story (it has its own
back-parser), and graded by the same grader.\footnote{On the literal arm
the text does fix which input is first, so a dualized answer is not a
faithful reading of \textit{this} text; the grader accepts and records it
anyway so that both arms are scored identically. Dualized answers are rare
in practice.}
The literal arm is the benchmark's built-in control. Sampling never
consults the rendering, so a story run and a literal run with the same seed
evaluate the identical pairs, and the gap between them isolates the cost of
the narrative indirection --- statement distribution, output notation, and
grading are all held fixed. The literal description is also, by
construction, what a perfect abstraction of the story would produce, which
is why it serves as the approximate ceiling for the two-stage protocol
studied in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.
\subsection{Difficulty, sampling, and scale}
Two structural dials set item difficulty: the total number of operation
applications in the pair (two to eight, once the zero-operation laws are
excluded) and the maximum nesting depth of any term (one to four). Because
law counts grow rapidly with size, uniform sampling is dominated by
maximal four-plus-four-application pairs; benchmark samples are therefore
stratified, drawing a fixed number of pairs per total-operation bin with a
seeded deterministic sampler, so any run's instance set is reproducible
from its seed and equal seeds give identical pair sets across arms.
The substrate is effectively inexhaustible: the 4{,}694 laws yield roughly
22 million ordered pairs, each renderable under four themes, and a seeded
synthetic generator can produce laws beyond the four-application cap in the
same format when a longer difficulty axis is needed. This bears directly on
contamination. The stories are novel text that exists nowhere outside the
benchmark; the mapping from story to statement appears in no training
corpus; memorizing the source project's published truth values does not
help, since the score rewards stating the claim, not settling it; and a
compromised sample can simply be redrawn. The marginal cost of an item is
one model call --- rendering and grading are free.


\section{Results}

\label{sec:results}
We evaluate a panel of contemporary language models spanning open and
proprietary systems and a range of sizes. Every run uses the seeded
stratified sampler, so the story, literal, and two-stage arms are scored on
the identical set of pairs and any difference between them is attributable to
the presentation alone; vacuous laws are excluded throughout, and every
number reported is the deterministic grader's verdict on the model's raw
output. We report the fraction of items judged \textit{correct}; the
remaining mass divides between \textit{wrong} (a well-formed answer that
denotes a different statement, i.e.\ semantic drift) and \textit{unparseable}.

\subsection{Abstraction helps improve translation accuracy}
Presenting a statement as a story rather than as a plain description makes it
substantially harder to formalize faithfully. Averaged over nine models on a
fixed set of 160 pairs, the correct statement is recovered from the story
$50\%$ of the time but from the literal description $67\%$ of the time
(Figure~\ref{fig:arms}). Because the two arms are rendered from the same pairs
and graded identically, this $17$-point gap isolates the cost of the narrative
indirection itself. The penalty holds for eight of the nine models---the lone
exception formalizes both presentations about equally well---and it widens as
models weaken, reaching roughly $20$ to $30$ points for the mid-sized open
models, while the strongest model in the panel stays high on both arms.

The gap all but disappears when the model is asked to abstract before it
formalizes. In the two-stage protocol the model first rewrites the story into
a plain, notation-free restatement of its logical content, and only then
translates that restatement into the target notation. This single
intermediate step recovers a median of $99\%$ of the story-to-literal gap
across the models for which the gap exists, raising pooled story accuracy to
$66\%$, level with the literal arm. Since the literal description is by
construction what a flawless abstraction of the story would yield, the result
localizes the difficulty of the story arm: it lies in seeing through the
narrative to the underlying claim, not in producing the formal notation.

\subsection{Reasoning significantly improves translation accuracy for all three arms (story, literal, two-stage)}
The narrative penalty is a function of how much the model is permitted to
think. Enabling reasoning lifts all three arms to near-ceiling accuracy and
collapses the differences between them: on a matched set of pairs across eight
models, story accuracy rises from $40\%$ to $97\%$, literal from $62\%$ to
$99\%$, and two-stage from $59\%$ to $96\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:reasoning}). The
advantage that the two-stage route confers without reasoning is thus subsumed
once the model can carry out the same abstraction internally over an extended
deliberation; the presentations diverge sharply only when the model must
answer immediately.

\subsection{Reasoning models spend more thinking tokens on story translations than on matched literal ones}
If reasoning equalizes accuracy across presentations, the residual cost of the
narrative surfaces instead as reasoning effort. On matched pairs with
reasoning enabled, every model in the reasoning-capable panel expends more
thinking on the story than on the equivalent literal description---by a median
factor of $1.4$, and up to $1.5$ for the strongest reasoners
(Table~\ref{tab:tokens}). The narrative, then, does not render the statement
unrecoverable so much as more expensive to recover: the model spends the
difference in deliberation and arrives at the same answer.

\subsection{Reasoning models spend more thinking tokens on more complex problems}
The deliberation a model invests scales with the structural complexity of the
statement it must formalize. Extending the difficulty axis beyond the
four-operation ETP cap with synthetically generated laws of up to ten
operations per equation, and letting five reasoning-capable models think
freely, we find that the median reasoning effort per item rises monotonically
with the number of operations---from roughly $1{,}270$ to $3{,}250$ tokens on
the story arm and $770$ to $2{,}090$ on the literal arm
(Figure~\ref{fig:tokens-complexity}). The story arm again costs more than the
matched literal one at every complexity level, and the gap between them widens
as statements grow. Over the narrow ETP range alone this trend is flat and
therefore invisible, which is why isolating it required the extended axis.

This deliberation is what sustains faithfulness as problems grow. Without
reasoning, accuracy collapses toward zero as complexity increases---story
accuracy falls from $82\%$ at one operation per equation to $0\%$ at ten---
whereas with free reasoning the same models degrade gracefully, still holding
$75$ to $81\%$ at the hardest tier (Figure~\ref{fig:cliff}). The
complexity cliff observed without reasoning is thus largely a budget effect:
given room to think, models pay proportionally more deliberation on harder
statements and remain faithful.
